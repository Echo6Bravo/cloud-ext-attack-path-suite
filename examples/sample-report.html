Executive Summary

This assessment identifies 4 internet-facing virtual machines across 3 cloud accounts that expose a network service carrying a serious, remotely exploitable vulnerability. Every path was confirmed against an observed listening endpoint (not a firewall rule), and every vulnerability is exploitable over the network and carries independent public evidence of real-world risk (high exploitation probability [EPSS ≥ 30%] or confirmed real-world exploitation).

  • Findings are split into two tiers by identity privilege — privilege is shown and tiered, not used to hide findings.
    • Tier 12 hosts run an identity flagged as having severe/administrative permissions [SeverePermissionActionPrincipalAttribute] — here a service compromise can escalate to broad cloud control, so these are the priority.
    • Tier 22 hosts are the same class of exposed, exploitable service on a standard-privilege identity — a real foothold, but contained blast radius.
  • The exposed attack surface is concentrated in a few internet-facing services — principally OpenSSH, Apache/nginx web servers, and (where reachable over RDP) Windows. Local-only and client-side vulnerabilities were deliberately excluded because they cannot be reached over the exposed port.
  • 2 host(s) carry a CISA-KEV vulnerability (confirmed exploited in the wild) — the highest-urgency subset regardless of tier.
Priority actions:
  • 1Remediate Tier 1 first — patch the exposed service and restrict its port to a bastion/allowlist, and replace over-privileged (editor/owner-class) machine identities with least-privilege roles.
  • 2Treat any CISA-KEV finding as immediate across both tiers.
  • 3Reduce Tier 2 exposure by closing or gating internet-facing services that don't need to be public.
How each path qualifies (plain-English gate, in order; underlying attribute in brackets). A workload appears only if all of the following hold, applied in this order:
  • 1it is a running VM [VirtualMachineStatus ≠ Stopped];
  • 2reachable directly from the internet [EntityNetworkAccessType = ExternalDirect];
  • 3open to a wide range of IPs [EntityNetworkAccessScope = Wide/All];
  • 4a live listening service was actually observed on an exposed port [NetworkEndpoint exists — ports come from the endpoint, never the firewall rule];
  • 5the finding is open [PackageVulnerabilityInstanceStatus = Open];
  • 6exploitable over the network [AttackVector = Network];
  • 7needs no unusual conditions [AttackComplexity = Low];
  • 8the vulnerable software is the service on the exposed port, not a local tool or client [component↔port correlation];
  • 9at least one public threat signal: high exploitation probability [EPSS ≥ 0.30] or confirmed in-the-wild exploitation [CISA KEV].
Signals intentionally not used as qualifying gates:
  • CVSS base / impact score— not used for qualification because CVSS is frequently overweighted relative to real-world exploitability and, applied as a threshold, admits more noise than signal.
  • VPR score— not used because this methodology targets the underlying exploitability signals (network reachability, low attack complexity, exploitation likelihood, and confirmed in-the-wild use) directly.
  • Proof-of-concept availability— not used as a gate because it tends to admit lower-impact and older findings that do not represent current, high-value exposure.
Notes. The published year shown per CVE is informational only and never affects inclusion; findings without a CVE identifier (e.g. distribution advisories) display a year of “—”. The stopped-instance exclusion is enforced at the query root and re-verified in post-processing so it cannot be inadvertently dropped. Because the threat-evidence gate relies on CVE-keyed public sources (EPSS and CISA KEV), findings without a CVE mapping are out of scope by design.
